Healthcare-Specific Forces

The economic, regulatory, competitive, and technological drivers

Economic

  • U.S. health spending hit $5.3 trillion in 2024 — about 18% of GDP.KFF, 2025
  • By 2024, roughly 78% of physicians were employed by a hospital or corporation, up from 62% in 2019.PAI / Avalere, 2024
  • Scale buys leverage: larger systems negotiate higher payer rates. The top reason practices sold was better payer rates (70%).AMA, 2024

Regulatory

  • Antitrust review reaches only deals above $126.4M, so sub-threshold roll-ups escape scrutiny.GAO, 2025
  • Medicare pays more for the same procedure in a hospital outpatient department than in a physician office — a standing incentive to buy practices.Bipartisan Policy Center, 2025
  • The ACA and the turn to value-based payment raised the cost of staying independent.

Competitive

  • In nearly half of U.S. metro areas, one or two systems control the entire inpatient market.KFF / Godwin, 2026
  • Hospital mergers have raised prices anywhere from 3% to 65% across studies — up to 40–50% in concentrated markets.RAND, 2022; FTC
  • Optum alone employs about 90,000 physicians — the country's largest physician employer — across insurer, provider, and pharmacy.Center for American Progress, 2024

Technological

  • Electronic health records cost a solo practice about $1,200 per user a year, but an 11-plus-provider group about $685 — scale the small cannot match.Software Path, 2022
  • Consolidation improved data exchange, but not interoperability — and not downstream outcomes.Chen et al., 2026

Predictable Costs of a
Predictable Pattern

Across the strongest independent evidence, the dominant pattern is consistent: prices rise, spending rises, and care does not measurably improve.

+12%
Hospital prices run 12% higher in monopoly markets than where four or more hospitals compete — and rise 6% when two nearby hospitals merge.
Cooper, Craig, Gaynor & Van Reenen · Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2019
+14%
The price of a physician’s services rises about 14% after a hospital buys the practice. Same doctor, same visit — a higher bill.
Capps, Dranove & Ody · Journal of Health Economics, 2018
$935B
Up to $935 billion is wasted each year — roughly 25% of all US health spending — in a system already near 18% of GDP. Opaque, uncompetitive pricing alone accounts for about $230 billion of it.
Shrank, Rogstad & Parekh · JAMA, 2019
#11
The US ranks last of 11 high-income countries on health-system performance — despite spending far more than any of them. After an acquisition specifically, deaths and readmissions do not improve.
Commonwealth Fund, Mirror Mirror 2021 · Beaulieu et al., NEJM 2020 · Borsa et al., BMJ 2023 (55-study review) · KFF 2020

Higher cost. No better care. Consolidation can create value inside a company without creating any for the market.

An Exception

The pattern has one. A few systems consolidated and the market got value—lower cost, better outcomes. The difference between them and the Bill is the whole argument.

Kaiser
Kaiser Permanente is the insurer and the provider at once, so it earns by keeping members well, not by billing more care. It holds top-quartile quality at lower hospital use.
McCarthy, Mueller & Wrenn · The Commonwealth Fund, 2009
Geisinger
Geisinger's ProvenCare put a warranty on cardiac surgery—one fixed price, with complications on Geisinger—and readmissions, complications, and length of stay all fell.
Casale et al. · Annals of Surgery, 2007

They did not integrate to own the price. They integrated to own the outcome, and were paid to keep it low. Same structure, opposite incentive, opposite result.
